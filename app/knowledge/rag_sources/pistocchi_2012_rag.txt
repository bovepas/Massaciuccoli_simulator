PISTOCCHI ET AL. (2012) — NITROGEN AND PHOSPHORUS CONTAMINATION IN THE MASSACIUCCOLI DRAINAGE NETWORK
Knowledge-base document for the Massaciuccoli Digital Twin
Source: C. Pistocchi, N. Silvestri, R. Rossetto, T. Sabbatini, M. Guidi, I. Baneschi, E. Bonari and D. Trevisan (2012). “A Simple Model to Assess Nitrogen and Phosphorus Contamination in Ungauged Surface Drainage Networks: Application to the Massaciuccoli Lake Catchment, Italy.” Journal of Environmental Quality 41:544–553. DOI: 10.2134/jeq2011.0302.

PURPOSE AND SCOPE
The paper develops and evaluates a simplified analytical model for nutrient transport in artificial surface-drainage networks where direct discharge measurements are limited or unavailable. The application concerns two mechanically drained subcatchments in the southern part of the Lake Massaciuccoli catchment: Vecchiano and Massaciuccoli. The model represents nutrient inputs from upstream reaches and groundwater, together with in-stream retention or release. It is intended as a descriptive tool for identifying nutrient sources, transport patterns, and potentially critical source areas under relatively stable flow conditions.

This paper reports a specific historical investigation. Its measurements and model outputs must not be interpreted as current conditions or as a general quantitative description of the entire lake basin.

STUDY AREA AND ENVIRONMENTAL CONTEXT
The study domain covers approximately 2,255 ha in the southern part of the Massaciuccoli Lake catchment and includes the Vecchiano and Massaciuccoli subcatchments. The wider lake and palustrine system is described as having shifted from an initially oligotrophic condition towards eutrophic or hypereutrophic conditions since the 1970s (as reported by the paper from earlier sources).

Since around 1930, much of the plain has been drained by artificial canals, ditches, and pumping stations. These systems transfer water from reclaimed areas towards the lake. During summer, approximately July to October, water flow can be reversed to supply irrigation water from the lake to cropland. The natural drainage network is described as poorly developed compared with the artificial network.

The mean annual rainfall is reported as approximately 900 mm, with typical autumn and spring peaks. The shallow phreatic aquifer consists of sand deposits approximately 30–40 m thick, overlain by more recent deposits that become progressively finer and more organic towards the north. Northern peat soils can contain up to 55% organic matter. The land surface has subsided following reclamation; the paper reports peatland subsidence of 2–3 m over 70 years and drained areas now approximately 0–3 m below mean sea level. Agriculture occupies about 80% of the study area.

A wastewater treatment plant (WWTP), designed for 4,250 population equivalents, discharges into the drainage network near sampling point 2. The study distinguishes this point source from diffuse agricultural and groundwater-derived inputs.

MONITORING DESIGN AND MEASURED VARIABLES
Water was sampled monthly at 12 locations along the drainage network during two distinct periods:
- Period P-I: February–May 2008; rainfall 274 mm.
- Period P-II: November 2008–February 2009; rainfall 809 mm.

Sampling was conducted under drainage/base-flow conditions and outside heavy runoff events. The authors considered monthly sampling a practical compromise for estimating representative concentrations in small streams outside storm-flow periods. Water samples were analysed for nitrate-nitrogen (NO3–N), orthophosphate-phosphorus (PO4–P), total phosphorus (TP), chloride, sodium, potassium, calcium, and magnesium. Field measurements included water and air temperature, pH, electrical conductivity, and alkalinity. Discharge and groundwater-head observations were also collected to support evaluation against an independent hydrogeological model.

The 12 locations were distributed along the ditch network according to accessibility, ditch connections, and the presence of point sources. Points 3 and 6 were downstream of the WWTP. Points 1, 15, 7, 8, 13, and 14 mainly drained cropland. Points 12 and 9 were outlets of the Vecchiano and Massaciuccoli subcatchments, respectively. Samples were also collected from the centre of the lake.

SIMPLIFIED ANALYTICAL MODEL
The drainage network is represented as a sequence of reaches bounded by sampling nodes. At each node, the nutrient mass flux is calculated from connected upstream fluxes, adjusted for flow partitioning where a node connects to multiple downstream reaches. The model then represents:
1. in-stream retention or release along each reach;
2. diffuse nutrient inputs entering from groundwater/base flow along the reach;
3. the resulting downstream mass flux, water discharge, and concentration.

Three principal parameters are used:
- a: unit groundwater discharge inflow rate per unit reach length (m² s⁻¹);
- b: unit nutrient mass inflow rate per unit reach length (mg m⁻¹ s⁻¹);
- c: first-order in-stream retention coefficient (m⁻¹).

The first-order retention formulation represents the decrease in nutrient mass flux as a function of reach length. The model also includes an upstream-flow repartition coefficient for network junctions.

The parameters are estimated inversely from measured concentrations using nonlinear least-squares fitting. Chloride and sodium are treated as conservative species (retention coefficient set to zero) and used to estimate groundwater inflow parameter a under steady-state assumptions. Parameters b and c are then estimated for non-conservative species, including PO4–P, TP, and NO3–N. Parameter values can vary spatially and between the two monitoring periods, but the authors constrained them to remain constant within each period to avoid overfitting the limited dataset.

The simplified model is evaluated by comparing estimated discharges with measured discharge and with MODFLOW-2000 predictions. Nutrient concentrations used in the fitting procedure are not an independent validation dataset; the paper explicitly notes that discharge is the output variable subjected to validation, whereas concentrations are fitted to the measured data.

Nitrate concentrations were significantly higher in P-II than in P-I (p < 0.01). The paper does not report a significant difference between periods for PO4–P or TP. Chloride concentrations were significantly higher in P-I (p < 0.01), consistent with differences in hydrological conditions and dilution between the periods.

The rainfall totals differed markedly: 274 mm in P-I and 809 mm in P-II. The authors discuss dilution as a likely influence on chloride concentrations. For phosphorus, dilution may have been balanced by leaching from soil stocks. Nitrate behaviour differed: lower concentrations during P-I may partly reflect depletion of soil nitrate following previous winter leaching, while peaks in April and May were plausibly associated with fertilisation of summer crops. Higher nitrate concentrations in P-II, when no fertiliser was applied, were interpreted as evidence that mineralisation of soil organic nitrogen in peat soils can be an important contributor to surface-water nitrate loads. These are interpretations offered by the authors, not experimentally isolated causal effects.

SPATIAL PATTERNS OF NUTRIENTS
Phosphorus:
- PO4–P and TP generally decreased downstream from the WWTP discharge point 2, where concentrations were high, with intermediate values at points 3 and 6 and lower values across much of the remaining network.
- Along the reach connecting points 14, 13, and 12, PO4–P and TP concentrations increased progressively towards the outlet in both monitoring periods.
- Concentrations upstream of the peri-urban area and in the Massaciuccoli subcatchment (points 8 and 9) were generally lower than in ditches affected by WWTP discharges.

Nitrate:
- Nitrate showed broadly similar spatial patterns in parts of the network, but points 8 and 9, which drain cropland in the Massaciuccoli subcatchment, had the highest concentrations along the ditches.
- The authors relate this pattern to higher mean nitrogen inputs reported for the Massaciuccoli subcatchment: 128 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ from fertilisers and cattle slurry, compared with 107 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ from fertilisers in the Vecchiano subcatchment. These are reported agricultural input estimates, not measured nutrient loads delivered to the lake.

Groundwater-derived phosphorus inputs:
- In the upper part of the subcatchment, estimated groundwater PO4–P concentrations ranged from 0.72 to 1.33 mg L⁻¹ in P-I and from 0.21 to 0.46 mg L⁻¹ in P-II.
- Near the pumping station, high phosphorus introduction rates were interpreted as likely reflecting high groundwater inflow rates.

RETENTION AND NUTRIENT TRANSFER
The model estimated mean phosphorus retention rates as follows:
- PO4–P: approximately 1.0 μg m⁻² s⁻¹ in P-I and 0.6 μg m⁻² s⁻¹ in P-II.
- Total phosphorus: approximately 1.18 μg m⁻² s⁻¹ in P-I and 0.95 μg m⁻² s⁻¹ in P-II.

The authors compare these estimates with published values from other agricultural and organic-soil drainage systems. They note that the higher retention estimates in P-I are consistent with the influence of lower stream velocity and longer residence time. These values are model-derived estimates for the studied network and periods, not direct measurements of a whole-lake phosphorus sink.

Phosphorus loads generally increased downstream, but some reaches showed decreases attributed to retention. The middle part of the Vecchiano subcatchment, especially around points 6 and 7 and the connected reaches, was interpreted as an important phosphorus buffer zone. The area upstream of points 6, 12, and 13 was also described as potentially buffering upstream nutrient inputs.

Nitrate retention was generally lower and more uncertain than phosphorus retention. Some reaches had negative values of the modelled change in nitrate flux per unit length (ΔF/L). Such negative values may indicate net release, but the authors considered inaccuracies in estimating groundwater-derived nitrate inputs a more likely explanation. High temporal variability in nitrate concentrations makes the assumption of constant inputs and retention within each monitoring period less reliable.

THREE BROAD SPATIAL ZONES AND CRITICAL SOURCE AREAS
The paper identifies three broad areas in the network with different phosphorus dynamics:

1. Southern Vecchiano subcatchment (points 1, 2, 15, and 3): substantial increases in nutrient loads, associated with high groundwater concentrations or high groundwater inflow rates depending on the monitoring period, together with the point-source influence of the WWTP.
2. Middle Vecchiano area (points 6 and 7, including connected reaches): phosphorus load reductions associated with prevailing in-stream retention processes; this area can act as a buffer zone.
3. Northern area (points 8, 9, and 13, including reach 13–12): substantial nutrient contributions from groundwater inflow to ditches, with possible influence from lake seepage.

For the Massaciuccoli subcatchment, high estimated phosphorus input rates were found along reaches 0–8 and 8–9. The paper also identifies groundwater-derived inputs as important in the northern network. These findings are specific to the monitored network and the two study periods.

MODEL PERFORMANCE AND LIMITATIONS
Estimated discharges ranged from approximately 0.03 m³ s⁻¹ in upstream reaches to 0.75 m³ s⁻¹ at drainage-network outlets. Mean discharge estimates were higher during the wetter P-II period (reported means 0.20 m³ s⁻¹ in P-I and 0.28 m³ s⁻¹ in P-II).

Comparison with MODFLOW-2000 and measured discharges showed:
- model efficiency: 0.96;
- coefficient of residual mass: −0.025;
- regression slope: 1.02;
- highly significant regression (p < 0.001);
- relative errors generally within approximately ±20%.

Point 8 had the largest relative error (−150%), associated with low discharge values (generally below 0.06 m³ s⁻¹) and greater uncertainty in estimating groundwater inflow parameter a in the upstream part of the network.

The model depends on important assumptions:
- surface runoff has limited influence on discharge and nutrient transfer;
- hydraulic and nutrient behaviour is sufficiently homogeneous between represented nodes;
- base-flow conditions prevail;
- water fluxes and nutrient input/retention parameters remain approximately constant during each modelled period.

The authors consider these assumptions more acceptable for phosphorus than for nitrate. Phosphorus patterns appeared mainly spatially controlled during the study periods. Nitrate concentrations showed greater temporal variability, making the steady-period assumption less realistic and indicating a need for more frequent monitoring.

The model is not suitable for predicting transient responses to changes in point or diffuse sources, including changes in cropping systems, soil organic-matter mineralisation, or wastewater management, particularly during rapid hydrological or nutrient-transfer events. It should therefore not be presented as a validated predictive model for management interventions or future scenarios.

MITIGATION OPTIONS DISCUSSED BY THE AUTHORS
For the northern area, where nutrient release from organic soils and peat mineralisation is considered important, the authors discuss:
- reducing nutrient release associated with organic-matter mineralisation;
- introducing perennial crops, forage crops, or saturated pasture;
- adopting soft-soil tillage techniques;
- rewetting low-lying areas;
- optimising fertiliser amounts and timing.

For the southern area, where wastewater quality is a concern, the authors discuss:
- improving WWTP efficiency;
- increasing the buffering capacity of the downstream drainage network;
- restoring riparian vegetation;
- redesigning ditch geometry.

These are proposed mitigation options, not interventions whose effectiveness was tested by this study.

RELEVANCE FOR SCIENTIFIC QUESTION ANSWERING
This paper is useful for answering questions about:
- point and diffuse nutrient sources in the Massaciuccoli drainage network;
- the roles of groundwater, agricultural land use, peat-soil mineralisation, and wastewater discharges;
- spatial differences in phosphorus and nitrate dynamics;
- in-stream phosphorus retention and the identification of buffer zones;
- the distinction between phosphorus and nitrate behaviour;
- limitations of simplified models in ungauged drainage networks;
- historical, study-specific evidence about nutrient contamination in the southern basin.

When using the findings, preserve the distinction between measured concentrations, model-estimated fluxes and retention rates, and the authors' interpretations. Do not generalise the two monitoring periods to present-day conditions or the entire lake basin.

DATA AVAILABILITY AND FAIR STATUS
The article has a persistent DOI (10.2134/jeq2011.0302). However, the paper does not identify a public repository or a separate persistent identifier for the underlying raw monitoring dataset. Some ARPAT groundwater concentration data are cited as unpublished data. No dataset reuse licence or complete public data-access route is documented in the article. The article DOI identifies the publication, not a separately deposited dataset. Therefore, the underlying monitoring data cannot be classified as verified FAIR data on the basis of this source alone.
