SOURCE
Pasquetti, F., Natali, S., Luppichini, M., Bini, M., Del Seppia, N., Delgado-Huertas, A., & Giannecchini, R. (2024). Hydrodynamics and water quality of a highly anthropized wetland: the case study of the Massaciuccoli basin (Tuscany, Italy). Environmental Science and Pollution Research, 31, 43117–43137. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-024-33899-2

DOCUMENT PURPOSE AND SCOPE
This document summarizes the study's observations and interpretations for scientific retrieval. It is not a basin-wide or lake-wide monitoring inventory. The field investigation focused on the drained areas in the SOUTH-EASTERN part of the Lake Massaciuccoli basin, specifically the Vecchiano and Massaciuccoli sub-catchments, their drainage ditches and the Barra canal. Results from individual monitoring points must not be generalized to the entire lake, all wetlands, or the full 114 km2 hydrological basin.

SPATIAL DOMAIN — REQUIRED CONTEXT FOR EVERY RESULT
The study area is the southernmost/south-eastern drained sector of the Massaciuccoli basin. Surface-water monitoring covered selected ditches in the Vecchiano and Massaciuccoli sub-catchments, the Barra canal, and two external points connected to the lake. Groundwater observations came from only two purpose-installed piezometers (PZ1 and PZ2) and one pre-existing well (W1) outside the drained sub-basins. PZ1 and PZ2 are not a representative network for the whole aquifer. The lake itself was sampled at one point (LM1) only during the June 2021 isotope survey. Chemical results from the ditches and wells therefore describe sampled locations, not the average chemistry or condition of Lake Massaciuccoli.

TEMPORAL DOMAIN — REQUIRED CONTEXT FOR EVERY RESULT
Surface-water field parameters (water level, electrical conductivity, temperature, pH and turbidity) were monitored monthly from October 2020 to October 2021. Groundwater levels were measured approximately fortnightly: PZ1 from September 2020 to September 2022; PZ2 from September 2021 to September 2022. Water chemistry was sampled on three dates only: 11 December 2020, 17 June 2021 and 1 October 2021. PZ2 was chemically sampled only in October 2021. Stable isotopes of water and dissolved sulfate were sampled in June 2021 only. Some planned ditch samples could not be collected because water levels were too low; therefore not all locations have data for all campaigns. These observations are a short, site-specific snapshot and do not establish long-term trends or current conditions.

STUDY DESIGN AND MEASUREMENTS
The study combined monthly field monitoring, groundwater-level observations, three hydrochemical sampling campaigns, stable-isotope analyses and multivariate compositional statistics (centered log-ratio biplots). Surface-water monitoring points were selected partly for accessibility and the presence of georeferenceable structures. Water levels were measured relative to surveyed reference structures. EC was reported at 25 °C. Major cations and anions, alkalinity and trace elements were analyzed; water isotopes were δ18O and δ2H, and dissolved sulfate isotopes were δ34S-SO4 and δ18O-SO4. The study's data tables report concentrations and detection-limit notation; values marked “<” are below the relevant analytical detection limit and must not be treated as zero.

HYDRODYNAMIC OBSERVATIONS IN THE SOUTH-EASTERN DRAINED SECTOR
During the October 2020–October 2021 surface-water monitoring period, ditch water levels were relatively stable. The Barra canal and points directly connected to the lake had levels around 0 m a.s.l.; the ditch levels generally declined from the south-eastern sector toward the pumping system. Southern points PT2 and PT15 had higher levels (approximately −1 and −2 m a.s.l., respectively) than several other ditches, commonly around −3 to −4 m a.s.l. Even when pumps were switched off, the observed ditch flow direction was generally toward the lake and pumping stations.

In the same study area, groundwater levels were more variable than surface-water levels and responded mainly to precipitation. Both piezometers generally had higher water levels than nearby ditches; PZ1 was higher than PZ2, consistent with its greater distance from the pumping system. This head relationship indicates that the monitored ditches continued to drain the unconfined aquifer during the observation period. The authors interpret the relatively stable surface-water levels as evidence of the effectiveness of pumping in maintaining controlled levels. This is an interpretation of the monitored system during the study period, not proof that pumping produces identical conditions throughout the basin or in all seasons.

The observed lowering hydraulic gradient from the south-east toward the pumping system is consistent with earlier hydrological and hydrogeological models for this sector (Rossetto et al., 2010). The article also describes a seasonal reversal of flow from the lake toward cropland for irrigation in summer as part of the basin's water-management system. Do not infer that a reversal was measured at every monitoring point during this particular monitoring campaign unless a specific observation supports it.

SURFACE-WATER FIELD PARAMETERS
Across the monitored ditches, mean electrical conductivity (EC) exceeded 1,200 µS/cm at all listed points; the highest mean values included PT6 (2,266 µS/cm), PT5 (2,729 µS/cm), PT8 (2,644 µS/cm), PT11 (2,923 µS/cm) and external point PT7 (2,645 µS/cm). Values above 2,000 µS/cm were generally observed at points closer to the lake and/or pumping system. These are point-specific monitoring-period summary statistics, not a lake-wide salinity estimate. Turbidity, pH and temperature also varied among points and over time; pH generally ranged from about 6 to 9. The article relates field-parameter variability to atmospheric conditions, but the short observation window does not establish long-term trends.

HYDROCHEMICAL FACIES AND SPATIAL PATTERN
Three principal hydrochemical facies were identified in the sampled waters: Ca-HCO3, Ca-SO4 and Na-Cl. Groundwater facies were comparatively consistent across the available surveys: W1 and PZ2 were Ca-HCO3, whereas PZ1 was Ca-SO4. The lake sample LM1, collected only in June 2021, had Na-Cl composition. Surface ditch samples were mainly Ca-SO4 in December 2020 and mainly Na-Cl in June and October 2021. In broad spatial terms, the facies shifted from Ca-HCO3 toward Ca-SO4 and Na-Cl moving from the south-eastern sector toward the lake.

Despite the Na-Cl facies in some surface samples, total ionic salinity of the sampled surface waters remained below 100 meq/L, substantially below seawater. The facies classification describes relative ionic composition; it does not mean that ditch water had seawater salinity. The authors interpret surface-water chemistry as reflecting several processes, including rock weathering, evaporation/precipitation and mixing with seawater. The Na-Cl association is consistent with a marine contribution, plausibly entering through the lake and its connection with the sea via the Burlamacca canal. Evapotranspiration and seasonal water-management conditions may also contribute. These are process interpretations, not direct quantifications of the seawater fraction at each ditch.

NITRATE OBSERVATIONS — THREE CAMPAIGNS ONLY
Nitrate concentrations (reported in Table 2 as NO3, mg/L, not as NO3-N) varied among sampled points and campaigns. All reported values were below 50 mg/L, the water-quality limit cited by the authors from the Nitrates Directive 91/676/EEC. The highest values occurred in the 11 December 2020 campaign: PT9 45 mg/L, PT5 35 mg/L, PZ1 34 mg/L, PT10 26 mg/L, PT6 25 mg/L, PT11 18 mg/L, PT4 27 mg/L and PT2 8.7 mg/L. In June and October 2021, many results were below the reported detection limit of 3 mg/L; some exceptions included PT9 (5.1 mg/L in October 2021) and PT11 (3.5 mg/L in June 2021). PZ1 was below 3 mg/L in June and October 2021. W1 was below 3 mg/L in all three campaigns.

The authors interpret the generally sub-50 mg/L nitrate concentrations as suggesting a limited impact of agricultural activity/fertilizer use on nitrate concentrations in the sampled waters, and they relate this to less intensive agricultural practices implemented in recent years. However, the December 2020 campaign had notably higher nitrate concentrations than the later campaigns. The authors' discussion also refers to higher nitrate values in December 2021, but the sampling table identifies the high campaign as 11 December 2020; use the dated table values when reporting the measurements.

CRITICAL INTERPRETIVE LIMIT: three discrete campaigns in a restricted south-eastern sector cannot establish annual nitrogen loads to the lake, prove that agriculture is no longer an important nutrient source across the basin, or demonstrate a long-term decline in nitrate pollution. Concentration is not load: load estimation requires discharge and temporally representative concentration data. The study does not provide a basin-wide annual nitrate or phosphorus load. The 50 mg/L threshold comparison is the authors' cited benchmark and should not be interpreted as evidence that all ecological nutrient pressures are absent.

TRACE ELEMENTS: Fe, Mn AND Ni
Most measured trace-element concentrations were generally low in the sampled waters. Iron (Fe) and manganese (Mn) were notable exceptions, with particularly high concentrations in groundwater, especially PZ1 and W1. Nickel (Ni) locally exceeded the Italian groundwater contamination threshold cited by the authors (D.Lgs. 152/2006) in some samples, including PT9, PT10 and PZ1. These are localized sample results, not evidence of uniform contamination across the basin.

The authors discuss Fe and Mn as redox-sensitive elements. Water-level oscillations and vertical movement of groundwater may alternately expose organic-rich sediments to oxygenated and reducing conditions, promoting organic-matter degradation and Fe/Mn mobilization; precipitation of Fe/Mn oxides under more oxygenated conditions may help explain lower concentrations in surface waters. Peat degradation associated with reclamation and agricultural activity is proposed as a plausible contributor, while agricultural inputs are also mentioned. The authors explicitly state that further work is needed to disentangle the factors and mechanisms responsible for Fe and Mn contamination. These mechanisms must therefore be presented as hypotheses/interpretations, not as proven causal attribution.

Ni appears proportional to Ca and Sr in the authors' compositional analysis, suggesting a shared source or shared controlling processes; the study does not establish a unique Ni source. Do not infer a specific industrial or agricultural source from this relationship alone.

WATER STABLE ISOTOPES: METEORIC ORIGIN AND EVAPORATION
Water δ18O and δ2H isotopes were measured in June 2021 only. The sampled groundwater and surface waters generally plotted near the Global and regional Meteoric Water Lines, supporting a meteoric origin. Groundwater at PZ1 and W1 had similar isotope compositions within analytical uncertainty. Their signatures were interpreted as reflecting recharge and mixing over longer periods rather than a direct response to the most recent rainfall.

The lake sample LM1 was distinctly enriched in heavy isotopes and had low d-excess: δ18O = −2.47‰, δ2H = −16.6‰ and d-excess = 3.2‰. This is consistent with evaporation of lake water. By comparison, June 2021 values included PT4 δ18O = −6.04‰ and δ2H = −35.5‰; PT9 −5.23‰ and −31.2‰; PT10 −5.12‰ and −31.3‰; PZ1 −5.48‰ and −33.1‰; W1 −5.42‰ and −32.2‰. PT4 was more isotopically depleted than groundwater; the authors suggest possible mixing with another depleted source such as Serchio River infiltration, but this is not demonstrated. The isotope evidence for evaporation and possible river contribution applies to this single June 2021 survey and these sampling points; it is not a continuous isotope record or a quantified water balance.

DISSOLVED SULFATE: MULTIPLE POSSIBLE SOURCES AND REDOX PROCESSES
Dissolved sulfate concentrations and δ34S-SO4 / δ18O-SO4 values differed strongly among the June 2021 samples. PZ1 had 1,697 mg/L SO4, δ34S-SO4 = −0.6‰ and δ18O-SO4 = 6.6‰. The isotopic signature falls within fields associated by the authors with sulfide oxidation, particularly pyrite oxidation. They propose that pyrite disseminated in peat deposits could be oxidized under changing water-table/oxygen conditions. Specific investigations of local peat deposits would be needed to validate this hypothesis. The high sulfate concentration in PZ1 is far above the marine sulfate contribution expected from simple seawater mixing.

W1 had 58 mg/L SO4, δ34S-SO4 = +40.8‰ and δ18O-SO4 = 19.2‰, together with absent/detectably very low nitrate and very high Fe and Mn. The authors interpret this combination as evidence of bacterial sulfate reduction and anoxic conditions at deeper levels of the aquifer. This interpretation is based on the W1 sample and should not be generalized to all groundwater in the basin.

Surface-water sulfate isotope signatures were intermediate and spatially variable. PT10 had 177 mg/L SO4, δ34S-SO4 = +16.3‰ and δ18O-SO4 = 11.0‰; its signature falls near fields associated with Triassic evaporitic sulfate and the Serchio River/cold springs, suggesting gypsum dissolution in geological formations as a possible source. PT9 had 36 mg/L SO4, δ34S-SO4 = +7.7‰ and δ18O-SO4 = 12.8‰; PT4 had 103 mg/L SO4, δ34S-SO4 = +14.4‰ and δ18O-SO4 = 7.5‰. The authors interpret the isotope shifts at PT4 and PT9 as potentially reflecting synthetic fertilizers and/or wastewater. These source attributions are suggestive, not definitive, because local isotopic end-members for all anthropogenic sources were not available and sulfur sources/processes overlap.

The authors estimate that seawater sulfate probably contributes only a small fraction of total dissolved sulfate in the ditches, while sulfate from precipitation is negligible; based on rainfall data from a site about 15 km north of the lake, precipitation could contribute at most approximately 5% of total dissolved sulfate in surface and groundwater. This is an author estimate based on external rainfall observations, not direct source apportionment at every monitoring point.

OVERALL INTERPRETATION AND LIMITATIONS
The study concludes that surface water and groundwater in the monitored south-eastern drained sector have meteoric origins, but their chemistry is modified by different processes. Groundwater chemistry varies spatially among the few sampled wells and is interpreted as reflecting local geology, peat-related processes and redox reactions. Surface-water chemistry varies in both space and time and is additionally affected by lake/sea-water mixing and evapotranspiration/precipitation. The authors emphasize that sulfur sources are difficult to disentangle in a complex wetland with multiple sources and variable redox conditions.

Interpretations concerning peat degradation, pyrite oxidation, bacterial sulfate reduction, seawater mixing, agricultural inputs and wastewater must remain attributed to the authors and expressed with the uncertainty stated above. The study does not establish a complete causal partition of solute sources, a basin-wide contaminant budget, a predictive hydrological or geochemical model, or ecological response functions. It does not quantify how a specified climate or management scenario would change lake water quality.

The spatial and temporal limits are essential whenever retrieving an individual result: (1) the main study domain is the south-eastern drained sector, not the entire lake/basin; (2) groundwater chemistry is based on two piezometers and one external well; (3) lake water chemistry/isotopes are represented by one lake sampling point and one June 2021 isotope campaign; (4) chemical sampling comprises only December 2020, June 2021 and October 2021; (5) water isotopes and sulfate isotopes were measured only in June 2021; (6) missing samples occurred where ditch water was insufficient; and (7) the data do not establish long-term trends. A retrieved finding must retain its monitoring point, date/campaign and the distinction between measurement and interpretation.
