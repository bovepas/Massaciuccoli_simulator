ABSTRACT
======================================================================

Species prevalence is a fundamental parameter in Ecological Niche
Models (ENMs) because it represents the prior expectation that a
species is common or rare within a study area before model fitting.

Reliable prevalence estimation is often difficult because occurrence
data are incomplete, spatially biased or temporally heterogeneous,
particularly for rare or poorly monitored species.

This study proposes a data-driven, multi-species methodology for
estimating species prevalence using publicly available occurrence
records from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF).

The methodology is completely unsupervised and combines two clustering
methods, one deep-learning model based on a Variational Autoencoder
(VAE), an ensemble model and statistical analysis to classify species
commonness and transform these classifications into prevalence
probabilities.

The methodology is evaluated on 161 species inhabiting the
Massaciuccoli Lake basin, a wetland of high biodiversity value and
ecological sensitivity.

The proposed framework is scalable, reproducible and provides objective
species prevalence estimates that can improve Ecological Niche Models
and biodiversity assessments.

======================================================================
INTRODUCTION
======================================================================

Ecological Niche Models (ENMs) estimate the environmental conditions
that support the persistence and proliferation of a species within a
specific area.

These models identify suitable habitats by relating species occurrence
records to environmental variables and can predict potential species
distribution under different environmental conditions.

The quality of ENMs depends on both the selection of relevant
environmental variables and the availability of reliable prior
information regarding species occurrence.

Species prevalence represents one of the most important sources of
prior information because it defines the expected commonness or rarity
of a species before model fitting.

======================================================================
ROLE OF SPECIES PREVALENCE
======================================================================

Species prevalence is formally defined as the proportion of presence
records relative to the total number of surveyed sites.

Within Bayesian Ecological Niche Models, including Maximum Entropy
(MaxEnt), prevalence acts as prior information that influences the
conversion of habitat suitability into probabilities of species
presence.

Species prevalence therefore represents the prior expectation that a
species is common or rare within the study area before considering the
available observations.

Variations in prevalence settings may influence habitat suitability
estimates, probability calibration and the resulting species
distribution maps.

Species prevalence also affects the conversion of continuous habitat
suitability predictions into binary presence–absence maps and
influences several performance metrics used to evaluate Ecological
Niche Models.

======================================================================
SPECIES COMMONNESS
======================================================================

Species prevalence reflects ecological characteristics associated with
species commonness and rarity.

Common species are generally widespread, frequently observed and
represented by numerous occurrence records.

Rare species are characterised by lower abundance, restricted
distribution or limited observations and therefore require particular
attention during ecological modelling.

Objective estimation of species commonness is essential for generating
robust, interpretable and ecologically meaningful Ecological Niche
Models.

======================================================================
LIMITATIONS OF CURRENT APPROACHES
======================================================================

Reliable prevalence estimation is often complicated by incomplete,
heterogeneous and spatially biased occurrence data.

Large biodiversity repositories contain valuable ecological
information, but observation frequency is strongly influenced by
sampling effort, temporal coverage, observer interest and taxonomic
bias.

Consequently, raw occurrence counts do not necessarily reflect the
actual ecological commonness of a species.

Many existing approaches therefore rely on expert judgement,
supplementary ecological information or manually defined prevalence
thresholds, limiting reproducibility and scalability.

======================================================================
OBJECTIVES OF THE STUDY
======================================================================

The proposed methodology introduces a completely data-driven,
multi-species framework for estimating species prevalence from publicly
available occurrence records.

The framework combines aggregated ecological features, unsupervised
clustering techniques, deep learning and statistical analysis to
identify species commonness and estimate prevalence probabilities.

The methodology minimises expert subjectivity, supports reproducible
prevalence estimation and provides objective prior information for
Ecological Niche Models.

The approach is demonstrated through a case study involving 161 species
from the Massaciuccoli Lake basin using GBIF observations collected
from Italian wetlands.

======================================================================
OVERVIEW
======================================================================

Species commonness describes the relative abundance and spatial
distribution of a species within a given ecological context.

Common species are generally abundant, widespread and frequently
observed, whereas rare species are characterised by lower abundance,
restricted distribution or limited observations.

Because species commonness directly influences species prevalence,
objective estimation of commonness represents a fundamental step in
Ecological Niche Modelling.

======================================================================
SPECIES PREVALENCE DISCRETISATION
======================================================================

Species prevalence is closely related to species commonness and can be
represented through a limited number of prevalence classes.

Discrete prevalence values simplify the incorporation of prior
ecological information into Bayesian Ecological Niche Models while
preserving the ecological distinction between common and rare species.

Prevalence discretisation also facilitates large-scale multi-species
analyses and reduces the influence of uncertainty associated with
incomplete occurrence data.

======================================================================
CHALLENGES OF LARGE BIODIVERSITY DATASETS
======================================================================

Large biodiversity repositories provide extensive species occurrence
information collected over different spatial and temporal scales.

However, observation frequency is influenced by multiple factors,
including sampling effort, observer activity, taxonomic interest and
survey duration.

Consequently, occurrence records represent only a partial observation
of ecological reality and cannot be interpreted directly as species
commonness.

Reliable prevalence estimation therefore requires methodologies that
can reduce the influence of these observational biases.

======================================================================
OBJECTIVE PREVALENCE ESTIMATION
======================================================================

Objective estimation of species prevalence should minimise subjective
expert judgement while preserving ecological interpretability.

The proposed methodology derives prevalence directly from aggregated
species occurrence patterns through unsupervised learning.

Species commonness therefore emerges from the structure of the data
rather than from manually defined ecological thresholds.

This approach improves reproducibility, scalability and consistency
across large multi-species datasets.

======================================================================
METHODOLOGY
======================================================================

The proposed methodology estimates species commonness and prevalence
through a fully data-driven, multi-species workflow based on statistical
features extracted from species occurrence records.

The framework follows the prevalence discretisation strategy introduced
in the Overview and assigns species to commonness classes before
associating each class with a prevalence probability.

The methodology is designed to classify species into three principal
commonness categories:

• very common;

• fairly common;

• rare.

These categories are associated with prevalence probabilities of 0.8,
0.5 and 0.2 respectively, which are subsequently used as multiplicative
weights within Bayesian Ecological Niche Models.

The workflow is organised into four principal stages:

• Feature Extraction;

• Modelling;

• Classification.

• Ensemble Integration.

An ensemble model combines the outputs of the individual models to
produce the final species commonness assessment.

 ======================================================================
FEATURE EXTRACTION
======================================================================

Species occurrence information is obtained from the Global
Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF), one of the largest publicly
available biodiversity repositories.

GBIF provides billions of occurrence records collected from scientific
institutions, monitoring programmes, museum collections and citizen
science initiatives.

The repository follows FAIR principles and adopts internationally
recognised biodiversity standards, making it suitable for large-scale
ecological analyses.

Because GBIF observations are affected by spatial, temporal and
taxonomic sampling biases, occurrence counts cannot be interpreted
directly as indicators of species commonness.

The proposed methodology therefore derives aggregated numerical
features describing species commonness within a specified geographical
area and temporal framework rather than relying directly on raw
occurrence counts.

======================================================================
FEATURE DEFINITIONS
======================================================================

Feature extraction requires the definition of:

• the geographical area of interest;

• the temporal interval considered for the analysis;

• the temporal aggregation unit;

• the spatial aggregation resolution;

• the list of analysed species.

From these inputs, numerical indicators are calculated to describe
species abundance, spatial distribution, temporal persistence,
observation frequency and dataset coverage.

These aggregated indicators constitute the feature space used for
species commonness classification.

======================================================================
MODELLING
======================================================================

The extracted features are analysed through three complementary
unsupervised methodologies.

Two clustering algorithms identify groups of species characterised by
similar feature values.

A Variational Autoencoder learns a latent representation of the feature
space and assigns a reconstruction probability to each species.

The outputs of the three models provide complementary descriptions of
species commonness that are subsequently integrated during the
classification stage.

======================================================================
CLASSIFICATION
======================================================================

Species commonness is assigned through a consensus procedure based on
the outputs generated by the individual models.

Species are classified into three principal categories:

• rare;

• fairly common;

• very common.

These commonness classes are transformed into prevalence probability
values of:

• 0.2 for rare species;

• 0.5 for fairly common species;

• 0.8 for very common species.

These prevalence probabilities constitute the prior probabilities
used by Bayesian Ecological Niche Models such as MaxEnt.

The methodology also supports finer prevalence discretisations by
introducing additional intermediate classes when greater resolution is
required.

======================================================================
CASE STUDY
======================================================================

The methodology was evaluated through a case study focused on the
Massaciuccoli Lake basin, a biodiversity-rich wetland ecosystem in
Tuscany, Italy.

To simulate the estimation of species prevalence before local
observations become available, occurrence records collected directly
within the Massaciuccoli basin were excluded from model training.

Instead, species occurrence data were collected from ecologically
similar Italian wetlands identified through the Ramsar Convention on
Wetlands.

A total of 161 species representative of the biodiversity of the
Massaciuccoli basin were analysed.

The GBIF dataset contained 6,027 occurrence records collected from
57 Italian wetland areas.

The resulting dataset provided the basis for estimating species
commonness and prevalence prior to applying Ecological Niche Models to
the Massaciuccoli basin.

======================================================================
EVALUATION METHODOLOGY
======================================================================

The methodology was evaluated by comparing the automatic species
commonness classifications with independent expert assessments.

Species were classified into three commonness categories:

• very common;

• fairly common;

• rare.

A complementary binary evaluation distinguished between:

• very common;

• less common.

Agreement between automatic classifications and expert assessments was
measured through Accuracy and Cohen's Kappa.

An expert ensemble was also constructed by combining the assessments
of the two experts through the same consensus strategy adopted for the
ensemble model.

In addition, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed by
removing one feature at a time and measuring the corresponding loss of
classification agreement.

This procedure quantified the contribution of each feature to the final
species commonness assessment.

======================================================================
CLASSIFICATION PERFORMANCE
======================================================================

The Variational Autoencoder achieved the highest agreement with the
expert assessments in both binary and three-class evaluations.

The best results were obtained with the expert ensemble, reaching
90.06% accuracy in the binary assessment and 85.71% accuracy in the
three-class assessment.

The corresponding Cohen's Kappa values indicated substantial agreement
between the automatic classifications and the expert consensus.

Overall, the VAE provided the most reliable representation of species
commonness among the evaluated methodologies.

======================================================================
MODEL COMPARISON
======================================================================

The three modelling approaches exhibited complementary behaviours.

Multi K-means showed a tendency to assign lower commonness levels,
whereas X-means generally produced higher commonness classifications.

The Variational Autoencoder achieved the highest overall agreement with
expert assessments, suggesting that anomalous feature patterns are
strongly associated with species rarity.

The ensemble model provided stable classifications by combining the
strengths of the individual methodologies.

======================================================================
FEATURE IMPORTANCE
======================================================================

The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis identified the most informative
features for species commonness classification.

Features describing intra-dataset record abundance, species abundance
per record and observation frequency contributed most strongly to
classification performance.

These indicators therefore represent the principal descriptors of
species commonness within the proposed methodology.

======================================================================
INTERPRETATION OF THE CASE STUDY
======================================================================

The Massaciuccoli Lake basin serves as a representative ecological case
study demonstrating the behaviour and interpretability of the proposed
framework.

The observed prevalence distributions and classification boundaries are
specific to this case study and are not intended to represent universal
ecological thresholds.

Instead, the case study illustrates how the methodology can be applied
to a real-world heterogeneous species assemblage while preserving the
generality of the underlying methodological framework.

======================================================================
DISCUSSION
======================================================================

The proposed methodology demonstrates that species prevalence can be
estimated objectively from publicly available occurrence data through a
fully data-driven workflow.

Rather than relying on expert-defined prevalence values, the framework
derives species commonness directly from aggregated ecological
descriptors extracted from biodiversity observations.

The resulting prevalence estimates provide reproducible prior
probabilities that can be incorporated into Bayesian Ecological Niche
Models.

======================================================================
INTERPRETATION OF THE RESULTS
======================================================================

The comparison among clustering techniques, deep-learning models and
their ensemble demonstrates that different unsupervised methodologies
capture complementary characteristics of species commonness.

The Variational Autoencoder achieved the highest agreement with expert
assessments, indicating that latent feature representations provide a
particularly effective description of ecological commonness.

The ensemble further increases robustness by combining complementary
information produced by the individual models.

======================================================================
ECOLOGICAL IMPLICATIONS
======================================================================

Reliable estimation of species prevalence improves the ecological
interpretation of Bayesian Ecological Niche Models by providing
objective prior probabilities before habitat suitability modelling.

Objective prevalence estimation reduces dependence on subjective expert
judgement and facilitates reproducible biodiversity assessments across
large collections of species.

The methodology therefore supports more consistent ecological analyses
for conservation planning and biodiversity monitoring.

======================================================================
LIMITATIONS
======================================================================

The methodology depends on the availability and quality of species
occurrence records.

Sampling effort, taxonomic coverage, temporal heterogeneity and spatial
bias may influence the extracted features and consequently the
estimated prevalence classes.

The resulting prevalence estimates should therefore be interpreted as
probabilistic ecological indicators rather than exact measurements of
species abundance.

======================================================================
TRANSFERABILITY
======================================================================

Although the methodology is demonstrated using the Massaciuccoli Lake
basin, the workflow is independent of the specific study area.

The same analytical procedure can be applied to any geographical
region for which harmonised biodiversity occurrence data are available.

Only the occurrence dataset requires replacement, while the feature
extraction, modelling and prevalence estimation workflow remain
unchanged.

======================================================================
OVERALL CONCLUSIONS
======================================================================

The proposed methodology introduces a reproducible, fully unsupervised
framework for estimating species commonness and prevalence from large
biodiversity occurrence datasets.

The integration of clustering techniques, deep-learning models and an
ensemble strategy provides objective prevalence estimates suitable for
Bayesian Ecological Niche Models.

The methodology reduces dependence on subjective expert judgement,
supports reproducible ecological modelling and can be transferred to
other biodiversity monitoring scenarios based on harmonised occurrence
data.
