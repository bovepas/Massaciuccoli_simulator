1. DIGITAL TWIN DRIVERS REPORT

2. ECOLOGICAL INTERPRETATION NOTES
These notes provide concise semantic descriptions of the variables. They help identify ecological context, but do not by themselves prove that a variable causes, explains, or mechanistically determines the target variable.

Use retrieved excerpts only when they genuinely support an ecological interpretation relevant to the reported variables and target. General statements about wetlands or climate change do not, by themselves, prove a specific mechanism for a particular statistical association.

SCIENTIFIC RULES

- Preserve the reported direction and strength of every association you discuss.
- The first driver in the report is the highest-ranked association. Never omit it from the interpretation.
- Address every driver labelled "strong" by name. You may group moderate associations or leave their detailed interpretation to the separate DRIVERS section, but never imply that an omitted association is unimportant or statistically insignificant.
- The report gives association direction and strength; it does not necessarily report statistical significance tests. Do not describe an association as statistically significant, significant, or not significant unless that specific conclusion is explicitly provided in the inputs.
- Describe statistical associations, not causes or effects. Do not invent mechanisms based on general scientific plausibility. For example, do not claim that one variable increases or decreases another unless that mechanism is explicitly supported by the supplied evidence.
- Avoid causal formulations such as "causes", "leads to", "results in", "contributes to", "drives", "influences", "affects", "impacts", "determines", "because of", or "due to" unless the supplied evidence explicitly establishes that mechanism. - Do not describe the target as increasing or decreasing merely because one associated variable has a positive or negative association. Interpret each reported direction separately, especially when the strong associations have opposing signs.
- If the supplied evidence does not establish a specific ecological mechanism, say so briefly and describe the association without proposing one.
- Do not infer that the reported associations represent observed environmental changes.
- Do not add variables, recommendations, rankings, numerical results, coefficients, percentages, counts, or delta values to the interpretation. These results are handled separately by the Digital Twin.
- Do not repeat the complete driver list. Explain the ecological meaning of the strongest associations rather than merely restating the report.
- Avoid generic ecosystem essays and generic concluding sentences.

The environmental variables used in this study originate from open
datasets published by the District Basin Authority of the Northern
Apennines, the public authority responsible for hydrogeological
protection and water-resource management across Central and Northern
Italy.

The Authority provides openly accessible topographical,
hydrogeological, environmental and territorial information for the
Massaciuccoli Lake basin.

The environmental dataset describes the ecological conditions of the
basin through variables that directly influence habitat suitability and
species distribution.

The environmental variables include:

• Evapotranspiration (ET)

Amount of water transferred from the land surface to the atmosphere
through evaporation and plant transpiration.

• Water Surplus (WS)

Amount of precipitation exceeding evapotranspiration, representing the
availability of water resources within the basin.

• Climatic Water Deficit (C)

Difference between atmospheric water demand and available water,
indicating potential drought conditions affecting habitats.

• Land Use

Spatial distribution of natural and anthropogenic land-cover classes.

• Vegetation Characteristics

Environmental descriptors related to vegetation cover and habitat
structure.

These environmental layers constitute the predictor variables used by
the machine-learning models to estimate species richness and habitat
suitability throughout the Massaciuccoli Lake basin.

[Source: paper2_rag_revised.txt | Section: ECOSYSTEM RISK ASSESSMENT (ERA)]
Ecosystem Risk Assessment (ERA) is a methodological framework for
identifying, characterising and spatially representing the likelihood
that an ecosystem undergoes a critical transition from one ecological
state to another due to natural or anthropogenic pressures.

Unlike traditional impact assessments focused on individual stressors,
ERA explicitly considers the simultaneous action of multiple pressure
factors, their interactions across different spatial and temporal
scales, and the non-linear responses of ecological systems.

In wetland ecosystems, ERA is particularly relevant because ecological
dynamics emerge from the interaction among hydrology, vegetation,
biodiversity, climate variability and land-use practices. These same
interactions also increase ecosystem vulnerability to cumulative
anthropogenic pressures and irreversible ecological transitions.
