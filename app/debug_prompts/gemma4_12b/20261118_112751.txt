Reed biomass and plant-derived detritus provide labile organic matter that can support microbial methanogenesis in anoxic sediments. 2. Dense reed beds shelter water surfaces from wind, hinder mixing and oxygenation, and can favour stagnant or hypoxic conditions. Reduced oxygen availability may limit aerobic methane oxidation during transport towards the water–air interface. The δ13C-CH4 values measured in Massaciuccoli, around -65‰ in the interpretation discussed by the authors, are considered consistent with a substantial contribution from acetoclastic methanogenesis and the availability of readily degradable organic substrates. The highest CH4 flux at LSW7 occurred in a site largely covered by reed beds. The authors interpret this as evidence of the dual role of reed beds: providing organic substrates for CH4 production and creating local hydrodynamic conditions that can favour CH4 preservation and release. The paper also discusses reed die-back syndrome (RDBS) and previous research suggesting that die-back areas can show enhanced sediment methanogenesis. However, the Massaciuccoli sampling campaign does not constitute a controlled experiment isolating the causal effect of reed die-back on emissions.
